## What we are actually offering

**Three laptops, working, in your building, at no cost, with no commitment.**

Not a trial that converts. Not a demo. Three machines that were dead, brought back, that you keep
whether or not you ever pay us. If they are still useful in a month, we talk about the rest of the pile.
If they are not, you have lost nothing and we have learned something worth more than the three laptops.

## Why this shape

A school cannot evaluate an operating system from a website. The question that actually matters to the
one overworked person who runs IT is not "is this good software" — it is **"will this create work for
me?"** That question can only be answered by machines sitting in their building for a few weeks.

So the first yes is a pilot, not a purchase order. Three machines working in their building is the sale.

## What we need from them

1. **Three of the dead laptops.** Genuinely dead ones — the pile nobody will miss. Not a machine anyone
   is still using.
2. **One conversation** about what the machines are for. Which three or four things does a student or a
   staff member actually need to do on them? That conversation is the recipe.
3. **Permission to write down what we find** about their hardware models, anonymised, in a public
   compatibility table.

That is all. No IT project, no migration plan, no meetings.

## What we do

1. Take the three machines.
2. Build them one image containing **only** what came out of that conversation — their language, their
   rules, their three or four applications, and nothing else.
3. Hand the machines back working.
4. Keep the image patched every night, for as long as they use it.

## What we will tell them plainly, before they agree

This is the part most vendors leave out, and it is the part that decides whether a pilot becomes a
customer or a complaint at month two.

- **Windows programs do not come across.** Not some of them — none of them. If the office runs a piece of
  Windows-only software, that machine keeps Windows or the software has to be replaced with something
  browser-based. We will tell you exactly which programs are on each machine before we touch it.
- **Files, bookmarks, Wi-Fi and printers do come across**, and we verify every file by hash before we
  change anything on the disk.
- **Some machines need one firmware setting changed by hand.** Mostly the 2012–2015 ones. We will know
  which before we start and we will tell you.
- **We are new.** We have no other customers to point at. What we have instead is that every recipe is a
  public file in a public repository — **if we disappear tomorrow, you can rebuild your exact operating
  system from that file without us.** That is the reason to risk three laptops on us, and it is the only
  reassurance we are entitled to offer right now.

## Who to approach first, and why

Ranked by how short the path is from "interested" to "three machines on a desk":

1. **A school or nonprofit already living in Google Workspace or another browser-based system.** The
   painful part of this product is the Windows software that does not migrate. An organisation whose work
   is already in a browser has almost none of that pain, so the pilot is likely to simply work.
2. **A computer-reuse charity or refurbisher.** They already have the pile, they already have the problem,
   and they evaluate operating systems for a living — which means they will find our weaknesses fast.
   That is a feature at this stage, not a risk.
3. **A public library or community centre with a bank of public-access machines.** These are the natural
   kiosk customer: a small number of machines doing a small number of things, where subtraction is
   obviously the right answer.

Deliberately **not** first: anything with line-of-business Windows software, anything with a compliance
department, anything where the machines are in active use. Those are real customers later. They are the
wrong shape for a first pilot, and a first pilot that fails costs more than one that never happened.

## The honest risk to us

The most likely way this pilot fails is not technical. It is that the three machines work fine, and then
nobody uses them, because nobody at the organisation had a reason to. **A pilot without a named person who
wanted these specific machines for a specific purpose is a pilot that ends in a cupboard.**

So the qualifying question before accepting a pilot is not "do you have old laptops" — everyone does. It
is **"what would you do with three more working computers next week?"** If there is no answer, we are not
solving a problem they have.
